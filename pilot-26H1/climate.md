# Climate and Environment — 26H1 Bulletin

*Period: 26H1 (2026-01-01 to 2026-06-30), covering developments published up to 2026-07-14. Latest data: NOAA Mauna Loa and global marine-surface monthly means for June 2026 (published 2026-07-07, preliminary). NOAA publication dates follow NOAA's usual release timing; the values are taken from a later NOAA file and may differ slightly from those first released.*

> *This endeavor is unlike the other four: it is about fixing the past rather than improving the future. Many historic civilizations fell to climate change, and we now have to clean up our mess if we are to survive into a better future.*

## Executive Summary

**Bottom line: world CO₂ emissions have stalled at a record high but have not yet turned down, so "The Bend" stays 🟡. CO₂ in the air set new records and is still rising far faster than a 1.5°C path allows, so the KPI is now 🔴 Worsening (previously 🟡). That rise has eased since the 2023–24 El Niño spike.**

**The good news**
- **Fossil power generation fell worldwide in 2025**, by 0.2% (-38 TWh). This was measured, not forecast. Renewables overtook coal as a source of electricity: 33.8% versus 33.0%[^ember-ger].
- **Emissions growth has slowed to a plateau.** The IEA puts 2025 energy-related CO₂ growth at around 0.4%, "the slowest rate since 2021"[^iea-ger]. China and India were roughly flat in 2025[^iea-ger][^cb-china-21m].
- **The rise in CO₂ concentration has eased.** Each month of January–June 2026 at Mauna Loa (MLO) was +1.48 to +2.26 ppm above the same month a year earlier. In May of 2023–2025, the year-on-year gains were +3.03, +2.90 and +3.61 ppm[^noaa-mm-mlo].

**The bad news**
- **CO₂ concentration hit a new record:** an MLO monthly mean of **432.34 ppm in May 2026**, and a daily mean of 433.95 ppm on 1 May 2026[^noaa-mm-mlo][^noaa-daily].
- **2025 emissions were a record on every fossil/energy CO₂ estimate.** The Global Carbon Budget (GCB) puts fossil CO₂ at 38.1 GtCO₂ (+1.0%)[^gcb-essd]. China's CO₂ then rose 2% year on year in Q1 2026[^cb-china-q1].
- **Time is running out for 1.5°C.** The GCB puts the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget at 170 GtCO₂, about 4 years of 2025 emissions[^gcb-essd]. The Met Office says CO₂ is rising too fast to track IPCC 1.5°C scenarios[^metoffice].

**What changed since the previous assessment.** In January 2026 the KPI was 🟡, based on the 2025 report's figures (426.5 ppm in November 2025; the 2023–2024 increase of +3.5 ppm, the largest on record). It is now 🔴 because concentrations keep setting records and the underlying pace remains far above what 1.5°C pathways allow. Growth has not accelerated. MLO concentrations set new monthly and daily records in 26H1. The 10-year trend of 2.56 ppm/yr shows no structural slowdown. Its dip from 2.64 ppm/yr (2015–2024) is mechanical: the El Niño year 2015 (+2.95 ppm) left the window and 2025 (+2.23 ppm) entered it. Half-decade MLO means still rose, from 2.51 ppm/yr (2016–2020) to 2.61 ppm/yr (2021–2025)[^noaa-gr-mlo]. The latest half-decade includes the 2023–24 El Niño spike, and the 2025 annual increase (+2.23 ppm at MLO) was well below 2024's (+3.33 ppm). The year-on-year slowdown in 26H1 is in the same direction as the Met Office forecast of a slower 2026 rise, which it attributes to La Niña-like conditions. Yet the Met Office says even that forecast rise (2.37 ± 0.55 ppm, Scripps annual-mean basis) is "too fast to track IPCC 1.5°C scenarios". Those scenarios need a 2020s decadal average of 1.33-1.79 ppm/yr, against 2.61 ppm/yr observed for 2020-2025[^metoffice].

*Basis note:* the 2025 report headlined WMO's +2.4 ppm/yr for 2011–2020, which is based on global annual means. This edition uses NOAA Jan 1 → Dec 31 growth rates. On that basis, the global 10-year trend for 2011–2020 was 2.38 ppm/yr[^noaa-gr-gl].

## Milestone Status

### 🟡 "The Bend" — Peak Global Emissions

**Status: 🟡 Approaching, not achieved** (assessed as of 2026-07-14; previously 🟡 "Approaching, not yet achieved", 2026-01-03).

**Rationale.** Every 2025 fossil/energy CO₂ estimate published in 26H1 is a new record; only GCB total CO₂, which includes land-use change, dipped. Growth has slowed to a plateau: global fossil power generation fell, and China and India were flat. The growth rates differ by source, though: IEA +0.4%, Carbon Monitor +0.7% and GCB +1.0%. The GCB's figure is slightly above the +0.8% it gave for 2024 in its November 2025 release[^gcb-nov]. But China's CO₂ rose 2% in Q1 2026, and the IEA expects the shock-driven 2026 fall in oil demand to rebound above the 2025 level in 2027. So the peak is not definitively behind us. *Caveat:* the milestone covers all greenhouse gases, but no global 2025 total-GHG estimate was published within 26H1, so CO₂ estimates serve as the proxy.

**What changed.** The status is unchanged. The January assessment rested on the GCB's November 2025 *projection* of a record 38.1 GtCO₂ (+1.1%)[^gcb-nov] and on China being flat or falling for 18 months[^cb-china-18m]. The final GCB paper (13 May 2026) keeps 38.1 GtCO₂ and gives +1.0%[^gcb-essd]; the IEA and Carbon Monitor now confirm 2025 records on their own bases; and China's Q1 2026 CO₂ rose 2% year on year[^cb-china-q1].

### 🔴 "The Balance" — Net Zero

**Status: 🔴 Distant — 53 GtCO2e/year to eliminate** (assessed 2026-01-03; not re-assessed this period, so there is no change).

**Rationale (January 2026).** Global GHG emissions were 53.2 GtCO2e in 2024[^edgar] and fossil CO₂ hit a record in 2025, far from net zero. Net-zero targets cover 77% of global GDP[^nzt-stocktake], but 2025 was a year of retreat (US Paris withdrawal, banks exiting the Net-Zero Banking Alliance, delayed corporate targets), and most IEA net-zero pathway benchmarks are off track.

No net-zero policy or target developments were gathered for 26H1. One related data point appears under The Bend: advanced economies' energy-related CO₂ rose 0.5% in 2025, the first annual increase since 2018 excluding the post-Covid rebound[^iea-ger].

### 🟡 "The Ceiling" — Below +2°C

**Status: 🟡 Under pressure** (assessed 2026-01-03; not re-assessed this period, so there is no change).

**Rationale (January 2026).** 2024 was the first calendar year to exceed 1.5°C above pre-industrial (WMO 1.55°C)[^wmo-2024], though the long-term Paris metric remains ~1.3-1.4°C[^wmo-climate]. WMO gives 70% odds that the 2025-2029 average also exceeds 1.5°C[^wmo-2024], and the 1.5°C carbon budget is "virtually exhausted"[^gcb-nov].

The milestone is +2°C, but the evidence available here concerns 1.5°C. These figures serve as early warnings for the 2°C ceiling, not as a direct measure of it.

**New in 26H1**
- **Remaining carbon budget quantified.** The GCB 2025 final paper (13 May 2026) put the remaining budget for a 50% chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C at 170 GtCO₂ (50 GtC) from the start of 2026, about 4 years at 2025 emission levels[^gcb-essd]. This updates the November 2025 "virtually exhausted" finding.
- **Concentration growth too fast for 1.5°C.** The Met Office said (4 February 2026) that even its forecast 2026 rise in Mauna Loa CO₂ is "too fast to track IPCC 1.5°C scenarios". Those scenarios require 2020s decadal average growth of 1.33-1.79 ppm/yr, versus 2.61 ppm/yr observed for 2020-2025, 1.91 ppm/yr in the 2000s and 2.41 in the 2010s (Scripps annual-mean basis)[^metoffice].

This report contains no 2025 or 2026 global temperature data, so the Ceiling status still rests on 2024 figures.

## Open Challenges

*The Open Challenges were not assessed this period; no status is given.*

### Net-Zero Transition

- **Fossil power fell for the first time since 2020** *(achieved)*. Global fossil power generation fell 0.2% (-38 TWh) in 2025, only the fifth time this century that it did not rise, as low-carbon generation (+887 TWh) outpaced electricity demand growth (+849 TWh)[^ember-ger]. Carbon Monitor puts global power-sector CO₂ at -0.9% for 2025[^carbon-monitor].
- **Renewables overtook coal** *(achieved)*. Renewables supplied 33.8% of global electricity generation in 2025 (10,730 TWh), against 33.0% for coal (10,476 TWh)[^ember-ger]. This confirms the IEA's earlier *projection* that renewables would surpass coal as the world's largest source of electricity by the end of 2025[^iea-ren-2025]. It came a year after global coal demand hit a record 8.8 billion tonnes in 2024 (IEA, earlier context)[^iea-coal].
