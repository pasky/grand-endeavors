## Climate and Environment

*This endeavor is unlike the other four: it is about fixing the past rather than improving the future — we now have to clean up our mess if we are to survive into a better future.*

*Narrowed scope: this run covers only the KPI and the milestone "The Bend".*

**KPI: Atmospheric CO₂ concentration (ppm) and 10-year trend (ppm/year)** — concentration **432.34 ppm** (May 2026 seasonal peak, Mauna Loa monthly mean; a new all-time monthly record); 10-year trend **2.56 ppm/yr (MLO) / 2.53 ppm/yr (global)** (average growth, 2016–2025). KPI assessment: 🔴 Worsening.

Short-term growth in CO₂ concentration has slowed, for largely natural reasons: the 2025 increase (+2.23 ppm at Mauna Loa, Jan→Dec) was well below the record years 2023–24 (+3.3 ppm each), and the UK Met Office links the slower 2026 rise to La Niña-like conditions temporarily strengthening natural carbon sinks. (Concentration growth is not an emissions measure, so this is not evidence of an emissions peak.) Separately, on emissions, growth has slowed to a near-plateau: GCB puts growth in total CO₂ at 0.3%/yr over 2015–2024, down from 1.9%/yr over 2005–2014. Ember finds fossil power generation fell 0.2% in 2025, the first fall driven by clean power. On energy CO₂, the IEA finds China fell 0.5% and India 0.1% in 2025 (India's fall largely due to a strong monsoon, per the IEA). Renewables overtook coal in global electricity on full-year 2025 data (33.8% share vs 33.0%).

**However:** Atmospheric CO₂ concentration keeps setting records: the Mauna Loa daily record was **433.95 ppm on 1 May 2026**, and the 10-year concentration trend shows no structural slowdown (MLO 2021–25 averaged 2.61 ppm/yr, against 2.51 for 2016–20). The Met Office forecasts the 2026 rise to stay "too fast to track IPCC 1.5°C scenarios". On emissions, every fossil and energy CO₂ estimate published in 26H1 puts **2025 at a new record**: fossil CO₂ 38.1 GtCO₂, +1.0% (GCB); energy CO₂ 38,082 Mt, +0.4% (IEA); fossil + industry CO₂ 37.2 Gt, +0.7% (Carbon Monitor). China's CO₂ rose 2% year on year in Q1 2026 (CREA), and any 2026 dip would be shock-driven: the IEA forecasts 2026 oil demand falling 1.1 mb/d after the Hormuz disruption, then a rebound of 2 mb/d in 2027. GCB puts the remaining 1.5°C (50%) carbon budget from the start of 2026 at 170 GtCO₂ — about 4 years at 2025 emission levels.
